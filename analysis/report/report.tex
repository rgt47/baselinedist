\section{Abstract}\label{abstract}

\textbf{Background.} Covariate adjustment in randomized clinical
trials is still contested in applied practice. This is so
despite a mature theoretical literature and clear guidance
from the FDA, the EMA, and ICH E9. This paper is a tutorial
review. It gathers that evidence into one set of practical
criteria.

\textbf{Methods.} We review the statistical literature, the
regulatory guidance, and the published empirical evidence.
Two small Monte Carlo studies illustrate known results. The
first compares unadjusted analysis, ANCOVA, and a
test-then-adjust rule for a continuous outcome. The second
compares an unadjusted risk difference with standardization
from a correct and a misspecified logistic working model.
Bias, coverage, efficiency, and power are reported. Monte
Carlo standard errors are given for bias, coverage, and
power, following the ADEMP framework.

\textbf{Results.} Eleven criteria are grouped under when to
adjust, which covariates to use, and how to adjust.
Prognostic covariates should be chosen in advance and
adjusted for. Stratification variables and the baseline
value of the outcome should be included. The decision to
adjust must not depend on observed baseline balance. A
simple rule links the number of covariates to the sample
size. The EMA and FDA differ on interaction terms and on
data-adaptive selection, and the criteria mark both points.
With a prognostic covariate, ANCOVA gained efficiency and
kept coverage near 95\%. Test-then-adjust had power close to
the unadjusted analysis and coverage above 95\%. A short
derivation shows this over-coverage is a selection effect.
Standardization from a working model that omitted a
quadratic term stayed nearly unbiased, at a small cost in
efficiency.

\textbf{Conclusions.} The criteria give the applied investigator
a route from trial design to the analysis model. They
consolidate existing guidance and are not new findings. Each
rule cites its primary sources. The simulations illustrate
the criteria but do not test them.

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Randomized clinical trials (RCTs) are the most rigorous
method for evaluating treatment effects in medicine. A
fundamental analytical decision in every RCT is whether
and how to adjust for baseline covariates in the
primary analysis.
Despite decades of statistical research and
increasingly clear regulatory guidance, confusion
persists. Investigators routinely test for baseline
balance between treatment groups. They then adjust
only if imbalances are detected. Some omit covariates
from the primary analysis to preserve `simplicity.'
Others select covariates post hoc, based on observed
associations with the outcome. Each of these practices
is suboptimal or outright harmful. We begin with a
review of the theoretical, empirical, and regulatory
evidence on covariate adjustment. We then synthesize
this evidence into a set of decision criteria for
when and how to adjust.

\subsection{The problem of baseline imbalance}\label{the-problem-of-baseline-imbalance}

Randomization guarantees that baseline covariates will
be balanced between treatment groups on average across
repetitions. It does not guarantee balance in any
individual trial. We note that\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-altmanComparabilityRandomisedGroups1985}{1}} showed that
even non-significant imbalances in prognostic factors
can strongly influence the observed treatment effect.
Such imbalances can render conclusions misleading.
Some investigators test for baseline balance and adjust
only when a difference is `significant.'\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-sennTestingBaselineBalance1994a}{2}} criticized this
practice sharply. He argued that it is
`philosophically unsound, of no practical value and
potentially misleading.' In Senn's view, significance
tests for baseline balance merely test whether
randomization was conducted properly. They do not test
whether observed imbalances affect the trial result.

Surveys of published trials confirm that these
problematic practices remain widespread.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-assmannSubgroupAnalysis2000}{3}} examined 50 trial reports
in four major journals. Most trials emphasized simple
unadjusted results. Covariate adjustment usually made
negligible difference, and subgroup analyses were
routinely overinterpreted. A companion paper by the
same group\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pocockSubgroupAnalysisCovariate2002}{4}} noted
the underuse of appropriate interaction tests. It also
noted the inconsistency with which covariates were
selected and adjusted for across studies. Among trials
using stratified randomization,\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-kahanImproper2012}{5}}
reviewed recent general medical literature. Only 14 of
41 eligible studies reported adjusting the primary
analysis for the stratification or minimization
variables. This practice leads to
overly conservative inference, with inflated confidence
intervals and reduced power.

\subsection{Theoretical foundations}\label{theoretical-foundations}

\subsubsection{Analysis of covariance in the linear setting}\label{analysis-of-covariance-in-the-linear-setting}

The classical statistical argument for covariate
adjustment rests on the analysis of covariance (ANCOVA).
Suppose the outcome \(Y\) depends on a baseline covariate
\(X\) and treatment \(Z\). The ANCOVA model is then
\[Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 Z_i + \beta_2 X_i + \epsilon_i\]
This model yields a more precise estimate of \(\beta_1\)
than the unadjusted comparison of means. This holds
provided \(X\) is prognostic for \(Y\). The variance
reduction is proportional to \(R^2_{Y|X}\), the
proportion of outcome variance explained by the
covariate. This gain is an average over randomizations.
It is not a guarantee for each trial. Conditional on the
realized allocation, the variance of \(\hat{\beta}_1\)
includes a term in \((\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_0)^2\), so a
chance imbalance in \(X\) costs some precision. Even so,
the case for adjustment does not require the groups to
be balanced\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-raabHowSelectCovariates2000}{6}}.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-sennChangeBaseline2006}{7}} showed that ANCOVA is superior
to the analysis of change scores (the `simple analysis
of change scores,' or SACS). When the linear model is
correct, the ANCOVA estimate is unbiased conditional on
the observed covariates. This holds whatever the
realized baseline balance. It resolves Lord's paradox in
the clinical trial setting\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-sennChangeBaseline2006}{7}}.

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-vickersBaseline2001}{8}} gave a concise tutorial confirming
that ANCOVA is the preferred analysis whenever a
baseline measurement of the outcome exists. It
yields estimates with smaller standard errors and
greater statistical power than either the comparison of
follow-up values alone or the analysis of change scores.

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-yangEfficiency2001}{9}} formally studied the relative
efficiency of several estimators in a pretest-posttest
trial. These were the change-score estimator, the
follow-up-only estimator, and several ANCOVA variants.
They showed that ANCOVA with a treatment-by-covariate
interaction term (ANCOVA II) and the GEE approach are
most efficient. Even
the simpler ANCOVA without interaction dominates both
the change-score and follow-up-only estimators. This
holds for all values of the baseline-outcome
correlation.

\subsubsection{The Freedman-Lin debate}\label{the-freedman-lin-debate}

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-freedmanRegression2008}{10}} challenged the use of OLS
regression adjustment in randomized experiments under
Neyman's randomization inference framework. He showed that adjustment can worsen
asymptotic precision and that OLS standard errors are
not generally valid. He also showed that small-sample
bias exists. These results generated considerable
concern.

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-linAgnostic2013}{11}} reexamined Freedman's critique. He
showed that the essential problem was the omission of
treatment-by-covariate interactions. Suppose a full set
of treatment-covariate interactions is included in the
regression. Then OLS adjustment cannot hurt asymptotic
precision, and the Huber-White sandwich variance
estimator gives valid standard errors. We note that in
balanced two-group designs even the simpler model
without interactions is asymptotically valid. Lin put
the reason as `two wrongs make a right.' That is, the
overadjustment for one group cancels the
underadjustment for the other.
Consider simple randomization with equal allocation.
The ANCOVA point estimate was already known to be
consistent there without assuming that the linear model
is correct\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-yangEfficiency2001}{9}}.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-wangANCOVA2019}{12}}
showed that its usual standard error is consistent as
well. Confidence
intervals and tests computed as if the linear model
were correct therefore remain asymptotically valid
under arbitrary misspecification in that setting.

\subsubsection{Semiparametric efficiency theory}\label{semiparametric-efficiency-theory}

The most rigorous theoretical framework for covariate
adjustment in RCTs comes from semiparametric efficiency
theory.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-tsiatisCovariate2008}{13}} and\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-zhangEfficiency2008}{14}}
developed a class of semiparametric estimators for the
average treatment effect. These estimators are
guaranteed to be at least as efficient as the
unadjusted estimator asymptotically. The size of the
gain depends on how prognostic the covariates are, and
it can be negligible. In a
randomized trial, any function of the baseline
covariates can be incorporated into the estimator to
reduce variance without introducing bias. This requires the estimator
to be constructed properly, using augmented inverse
probability weighting or equivalent approaches.

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-rosenblumRegression2009}{15}} proved a result for a large
class of commonly used regression models, including
linear and logistic regression. For these models,
standard hypothesis tests using robust (sandwich)
variance estimators are guaranteed to have correct Type
I error asymptotically. This holds even when the
regression model is misspecified. It provides
theoretical justification for the common practice of
fitting a potentially misspecified working model and
using robust standard errors for testing. The companion
result for estimation is due to\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-rosenblumGLM2010}{16}}. They gave simple estimators of
marginal treatment effects from generalized linear
working models that remain consistent under
misspecification in a randomized trial.

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-mooreCovariate2009}{17}} extended these ideas to binary
outcomes using targeted maximum likelihood estimation
(TMLE). The marginal risk difference can be estimated
efficiently by fitting a logistic regression model and
then marginalizing, or standardizing, over the
covariate distribution. This approach avoids a
well-known problem identified by\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-robinsonCovariate1991}{18}}.
That is, covariate adjustment in logistic regression
increases the standard error of the conditional odds
ratio. Robinson and Jewell also showed the other half
of the story. Power still increases, because the
conditional odds ratio lies further from the null than
the marginal one. As\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-mooreCovariate2009}{17}} and\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-tsiatisCovariate2008}{13}} later
clarified, this problem does not arise when the target
is the marginal effect.

\subsubsection{Survival outcomes}\label{survival-outcomes}

Covariate adjustment in the survival analysis setting
has its own theoretical development.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-luLogRank2008}{19}}
showed how auxiliary covariates can be incorporated
into the log-rank test to improve efficiency.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-yeLogRank2024}{20}} proposed a nonparametric
covariate-adjusted log-rank test with a simple closed
form. This test achieves guaranteed efficiency gains
over the unadjusted test. It applies universally across
simple and covariate-adaptive randomization schemes.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-diazSurvival2019}{21}} developed improved estimators for the
restricted mean survival time. These estimators use
baseline covariates without assuming proportional
hazards.

\subsection{Regulatory guidance}\label{regulatory-guidance}

\subsubsection{ICH E9 (1999) and E9(R1) (2019)}\label{ich-e9-1999-and-e9r1-2019}

The ICH E9 guideline\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ichE9Statistical1999}{22}}, in its
section 5.7, asks that pre-trial deliberations `identify
those covariates and factors expected to have an
important influence on the primary variable(s).' It
asks them to `consider how to account for these in the
analysis in order to improve precision and to
compensate for any lack of balance between treatment
groups.' The second purpose sits in some tension with\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-sennTestingBaselineBalance1994a}{2}}. Senn's view is that
adjustment is justified by prognostic value, not by
observed imbalance. We read the ICH clause as
consistent with pre-specified adjustment, which handles
any chance imbalance automatically. It should not be
read as license to adjust in response to an observed
imbalance. The same section also says that when the
value of an adjustment is in doubt, the unadjusted
analysis may be nominated as primary. The E9(R1)
addendum\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ichE9R1Estimands2019}{23}} introduced the
estimand framework. This framework requires precise
definition of the treatment effect of interest. It
makes the distinction between conditional and marginal
effects particularly relevant for covariate adjustment.
We return to this point below.

\subsubsection{EMA guideline (2015)}\label{ema-guideline-2015}

The European Medicines Agency guideline\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-emaBaselineCovariates2015}{24}} makes concrete
recommendations. First, the primary analysis should
include only pre-specified covariates. Second, no
treatment-by-covariate interaction terms should appear
in the primary model. Third, stratification variables
used in the randomization should generally be included
as covariates. Fourth, covariates should be measured
before randomization.

\subsubsection{FDA guidance (2023)}\label{fda-guidance-2023}

The FDA's final guidance on covariate adjustment\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-fdaCovariateAdjustment2023}{25}} is the most comprehensive
regulatory position to date. The guidance is
nonbinding. We paraphrase its main points closely:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  An unadjusted primary analysis is acceptable.
  Covariate-adjusted analyses should be specified before
  any unblinding of comparative data.
\item
  Covariate adjustment through a linear model, fitted
  by ordinary least squares, is `an acceptable method'
  for estimating the average treatment effect, even
  when the model is misspecified.
\item
  Nominal (model-based) standard errors are acceptable
  in two-arm trials with 1:1 randomization. In other
  settings, sponsors should consider a robust
  (sandwich) standard error or a bootstrap.
\item
  The linear model may include treatment-by-covariate
  interaction terms. The primary analysis can still be
  based on the model's estimate of the average
  treatment effect.
\item
  The adjustment model should generally include the
  stratification variables. Standard errors should
  account for stratified randomization, since ignoring
  it tends to overstate them.
\item
  Sponsors should discuss proposals for data-adaptive
  covariate selection with the review division. The
  guidance does not prohibit such selection.
\item
  For nonlinear models, the sponsor should state whether
  the target is a conditional or an unconditional
  treatment effect. Standardization (g-computation)
  from a logistic working model is given as `one
  reliable method' for an unconditional effect with a
  binary outcome. Inverse probability weighting is
  another.
\end{itemize}

The guidance's reference list includes\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-tsiatisCovariate2008}{13}},\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-colantuoniPrognostic2015}{26}},\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-rosenblumRegression2009}{15}},\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-rosenblumGLM2010}{16}},\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-freedmanLogistic2008}{27}},\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-steingrimssonBinary2017}{28}}, and\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-bugniCAR2018}{29}}, among others.

\subsection{The conditional versus marginal distinction}\label{the-conditional-versus-marginal-distinction}

A critical conceptual issue for nonlinear models is
the distinction between conditional and marginal
treatment effects. In a logistic regression model, the
conditional odds ratio (adjusted for covariates) is not
equal to the marginal odds ratio. The marginal odds
ratio averages over the covariate distribution. The two
differ even if there is no confounding or effect
modification. This `noncollapsibility' has generated
confusion about whether covariate adjustment `changes
the estimand.'

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-permuttCovariates2020}{30}} addressed this question
directly. He argued that adjustments can be used with
minimal assumptions if the target parameter is
carefully defined. On this view, the benefit of
increased efficiency comes at essentially no cost.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-vanLanckerCovariate2024}{31}} reviewed the available methods
and advocated the standardization approach. This
approach uses the covariate-outcome relationship for
efficiency while targeting a well-defined marginal
effect. In a commentary on this work,\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-harrellCommentary2024}{32}} agreed that covariate adjustment
`accounts for outcome heterogeneity and prevents power
loss.' On the other hand, he cautioned against the
marginal/standardization framing. He argued that
conditional models preserve the full information
content of the covariates. He also argued that marginal
effects can obscure treatment effect heterogeneity.

\subsection{Myths about randomization that bear on adjustment}\label{myths-about-randomization-that-bear-on-adjustment}

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-sennSevenMyths2013}{33}} listed seven common misconceptions
about randomization. Three of them bear directly on
covariate adjustment. We give them here unnumbered;
they are not consecutive in Senn's list.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Balance is necessary for valid inference.} False:
  randomization justifies inference regardless of
  balance; adjustment is for efficiency, not validity.
\item
  \emph{Observed covariates may be ignored because one
  has randomized.} False: ignoring prognostic
  covariates wastes information and reduces power.
\item
  \emph{Randomization is inefficient.} False: combined
  with appropriate analysis, randomization plus
  covariate adjustment is highly efficient.
\end{itemize}

These myths persist in part because introductory
statistics courses emphasize the role of randomization
in eliminating confounding. Unfortunately, they say
little about its role in enabling efficient estimation.

\subsection{Empirical evidence on efficiency gains}\label{empirical-evidence-on-efficiency-gains}

Multiple empirical studies have quantified the
practical benefits of covariate adjustment.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hernandezPower2004}{34}} used simulation to study trials
with binary outcomes. They showed that pre-specified
adjustment for prognostic covariates can reduce
required sample sizes by 3\% to 46\%. The size of this
reduction depends on the strength of the
covariate-outcome association.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hernandezImpact2006}{35}}
showed a 25\% reduction in sample size requirements in
traumatic brain injury (TBI) trials. This reduction
came from adjusting for seven baseline predictors in
the IMPACT dataset.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-turnerCovariate2012}{36}} reanalyzed
14-day mortality in 9497 participants of the CRASH
trial, which randomized 10,008 patients. They found
that covariate adjustment using validated prognostic
models substantially increased power.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-steyerbergHeterogeneity2004}{37}} showed that the difference
between adjusted and unadjusted effects can be
substantial in the presence of outcome heterogeneity.
In that setting, adjustment gives a more accurate
estimate of the treatment benefit.

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-colantuoniPrognostic2015}{26}} showed efficiency gains
equivalent to a 22-30\% reduction in sample size in
simulations.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-benkeserCovid2021}{38}} applied semiparametric
methods to COVID-19 treatment trials. They obtained
meaningful precision gains for binary, ordinal, and
time-to-event outcomes.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-schulerPrognostic2022}{39}} proposed
a method for adjusting via a prognostic score trained on
external (historical) data. They showed that the
approach attains the semiparametric efficiency bound
under certain conditions.

\subsection{Practical guidance for covariate selection}\label{practical-guidance-for-covariate-selection}

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-raabHowSelectCovariates2000}{6}} gave the first systematic
framework for selecting covariates. Their main
recommendations are as follows. First, covariates
balanced by design (stratification factors) should
always be included. Second, strongly prognostic
covariates should be included regardless of balance.
Third, data-dependent selection methods based on the
current trial data should be avoided. Such methods
produce misleading inference. Fourth, all decisions
should be pre-specified in the protocol based on prior
knowledge.

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morrisPlanning2022}{40}} offered a comprehensive practical
guide for planning covariate adjustment in individually
randomized trials. They compared three approaches:
direct adjustment, standardization, and inverse
probability of treatment weighting. All three are
asymptotically efficient and similar in large samples.
For marginal estimands, they advised against direct
adjustment because of convergence problems. They noted
that convergence is most likely with inverse
probability weighting. They also concluded that no
single approach is always best.

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-beachCovariates1989}{41}} addressed the question of how many
covariates to include. The benefits of adjustment
diminish, and can reverse, when too many weakly
prognostic covariates are added relative to the sample
size.

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-hauckCovariates1998}{42}} examined adjustment in nonlinear
models. They confirmed that the standard advice for
linear models does not always transfer. Care is needed
with the choice of estimand and variance estimation.

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-yePractice2023}{43}} gave a synthesis linking
covariate-adaptive randomization, stratification, and
covariate-adjusted analysis. It also offered practical
recommendations for modern trial design and analysis.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-bugniCAR2018}{29}} and\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-yeMinimization2022}{44}} showed why
stratification matters for inference. Under
covariate-adaptive randomization, the usual standard
errors that ignore the strata are generally
conservative. Valid and less conservative inference
follows when the analysis accounts for the
randomization scheme.

\subsection{Present study}\label{present-study}

The preceding literature provides a strong theoretical
and empirical foundation. Several sources already give
practitioner-facing recommendations that overlap
substantially with what follows. These include the FDA
and EMA guidance documents themselves and\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morrisPlanning2022}{40}}'s practical guide to planning
adjustment. They also include\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-kahanRisks2014}{45}}'s
empirical assessment of the consequences of (not)
adjusting. Finally, they include\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-vanLanckerCovariate2024}{31}}'s review of
conditional-versus-marginal considerations. We note
that this paper does not claim a new empirical finding
or a novel estimator. Its contribution is a single,
citation-backed decision procedure. The procedure maps
these dispersed sources onto three operational
questions: when to adjust, which covariates to adjust
for, and how to specify the adjustment. Relative to the
sources above, it adds three things. It gives one
ordered list with the conditions under which each rule
applies. It gives an explicit break-even rule for the
number of covariates. It also marks where the EMA and
FDA documents differ. We pair this procedure with a
worked illustration of why the common test-then-adjust
heuristic fails. That failure is not a new finding. It
was analyzed by\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-permuttImbalance1990}{46}} and argued by\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-sennTestingBaselineBalance1994a}{2}}; our simulation only
illustrates it. We synthesize the
evidence into these decision rules. We then illustrate
the efficiency and coverage consequences through a
small Monte Carlo simulation.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{Simulation design}\label{simulation-design}

We conduct two Monte Carlo simulations. Both are
reported following the ADEMP framework of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morrisUsingSimulation2019}{47}} (Aims, Data-generating
mechanisms, Estimands, Methods, Performance measures).
The first illustrates the efficiency gains from ANCOVA
relative to the unadjusted comparison of means for a
continuous outcome. It does so across a range of
covariate-outcome correlations. It also examines the
`test-then-adjust' strategy. Under this strategy the
analyst tests for baseline balance and adjusts only
when a significant imbalance is detected. The
simulation shows that this strategy does not improve
inferential properties and can in fact distort
coverage. It varies the covariate-outcome correlation
(\(\gamma\)) and sample size (\(n\)) only. We note that it
does not vary baseline imbalance as a design factor. It
is also restricted to a single continuous outcome and a
correctly specified adjustment model (see Limitations).
The second scenario (binary outcome under model
misspecification, below) adds a binary outcome and a
deliberately misspecified working model. Its purpose is
to illustrate criterion 8. Both simulations illustrate
known results; they are not designed to test the
criteria.

\subsection{Data generating model}\label{data-generating-model}

For each replication \(b = 1, \ldots, B\):

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  Generate \(n\) subjects, randomized 1:1 to treatment
  (\(Z = 1\)) or control (\(Z = 0\)).
\item
  Generate a baseline covariate
  \(X_i \sim \text{Normal}(0, 1)\).
\item
  Generate the outcome as
  \[Y_i = \beta_0 + \tau Z_i + \gamma X_i +
  \epsilon_i, \quad \epsilon_i \sim
  \text{Normal}(0, \sigma^2)\]
  Here \(\tau = 0.3\) is the true treatment effect, and
  \(\gamma\) controls the strength of the prognostic
  covariate. We set \(\sigma^2 = 1 - \gamma^2\) so that
  the within-arm variance of \(Y\) is 1. The marginal
  variance also includes the treatment term and equals
  \(1 + \tau^2/4 = 1.0225\).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Analysis strategies}\label{analysis-strategies}

For each simulated dataset, we apply three analysis
strategies:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Unadjusted}: Two-sample \(t\)-test comparing
  treatment group means.
\item
  \textbf{Always-adjust (ANCOVA)}: Linear regression of
  \(Y\) on \(Z\) and \(X\), with robust (HC2) standard
  errors.
\item
  \textbf{Test-then-adjust}: First test \(H_0: \bar{X}_1 =
  \bar{X}_0\) at \(\alpha = 0.10\). If rejected, use
  ANCOVA; otherwise use the unadjusted analysis.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Metrics}\label{metrics}

For each scenario and analysis strategy, we compute:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Bias}: Mean \(\hat{\tau} - \tau\)
\item
  \textbf{Empirical SE}: Standard deviation of
  \(\hat{\tau}\) across replications
\item
  \textbf{Mean model SE}: Mean of estimated standard
  errors across replications
\item
  \textbf{Coverage}: Proportion of 95\% CIs containing
  \(\tau\)
\item
  \textbf{Power}: Proportion of replications rejecting
  \(H_0: \tau = 0\) at \(\alpha = 0.05\) (two-sided)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Binary outcome under model misspecification}\label{binary-outcome-under-model-misspecification}

The scenario above covers only a continuous outcome
under a correctly specified linear model. We add a
second scenario to illustrate criterion 8
(standardization for binary outcomes) under one mild
form of working-model misspecification. Both
standardization estimators use the same HC2 sandwich
variance, and there is no model-based comparator. As a
result, the scenario says nothing about the choice of
standard error in criterion 10.

\subsubsection{Data generating model}\label{data-generating-model-1}

For each replication, \(n\) subjects are randomized 1:1
to \(Z \in \{0, 1\}\). A baseline covariate
\(X_i \sim \text{Normal}(0, 1)\) is also generated. The
binary outcome is
\[\text{logit}\{P(Y_i = 1)\} = \beta_0 + \tau Z_i + \gamma X_i
+ \gamma_2 X_i^2,\]
with \(\beta_0 = -0.5\), \(\tau = 0.6\) (log-odds treatment
effect), and \(\gamma = 0.8\) (linear log-odds covariate
effect). The quadratic term \(\gamma_2 X_i^2\) is the true
data-generating nonlinearity. It also defines the
misspecification. That is, one of the two working
models below omits it entirely. The estimand is the
marginal risk difference \(E[Y(1)] - E[Y(0)]\). We obtain
it once by numerical integration of the true model over
the known \(X\) density. It is not estimated from
simulated data, because \(\tau\) is a conditional
log-odds parameter. Under a nonlinear model it does
not by itself equal the marginal risk difference.

\subsubsection{Analysis strategies}\label{analysis-strategies-1}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Unadjusted}: difference in sample proportions
  between arms, with the usual normal-approximation
  standard error.
\item
  \textbf{Standardized (correct)}: fit
  \(\text{logit}\{P(Y_i=1)\} = b_0 + b_1 Z_i + b_2 X_i +
  b_3 X_i^2\) by maximum likelihood. Then predict
  \(P(Y_i=1)\) under the counterfactuals \(Z_i = 1\) and
  \(Z_i = 0\) for every subject. Average each set of
  predictions over the observed covariate distribution,
  and difference the two averages (marginal
  standardization / g-computation). The standard error
  is obtained by the delta method. It combines a
  numerical gradient of the standardized contrast with
  an HC2 sandwich covariance matrix for
  \((b_0, b_1, b_2, b_3)\).
\item
  \textbf{Standardized (misspecified)}: identical procedure,
  but the working model omits the quadratic term
  (\(\text{logit}\{P(Y_i=1)\} = b_0 + b_1 Z_i + b_2 X_i\)).
  As a result, the fitted conditional model is wrong
  whenever \(\gamma_2 \neq 0\).
\end{enumerate}

Both standardization estimators target the same
marginal risk difference. Identifiability is not in
question here; the issue is whether the estimator is
consistent when the working model is wrong. The
standard argument runs as follows\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-rosenblumGLM2010}{16},\citeproc{ref-freedmanLogistic2008}{27},\citeproc{ref-steingrimssonBinary2017}{28}}. Each working model is a
canonical-link (logistic) model with an intercept and a
treatment indicator, fitted by maximum likelihood. Its
score equations therefore force the mean fitted value
within each arm to equal the observed mean in that arm.
Randomization makes the covariate distribution the same
in both arms. Averaging the counterfactual predictions
over the whole sample is then consistent for each arm's
marginal mean, whether or not the conditional model is
correct.

Table \ref{tab:results-table} presents the simulation
results. All simulation figures are read directly from
the saved simulation output; none are hard-coded. Consider
first a nonprognostic covariate (\(\gamma = 0\)). We
observe that all three strategies perform similarly in
this case, as expected. ANCOVA's empirical SE is
slightly larger than the unadjusted one here
(0.1987 versus
0.1974 at \(n = 100\)).
This small loss reflects the degree of freedom spent on
a useless covariate. As
\(\gamma\) increases, ANCOVA achieves progressively
smaller empirical standard errors, with correspondingly
higher power. Consider \(\gamma = 0.7\). In this case the
covariate explains 49\% of outcome variance. ANCOVA
increases power from 0.32
to 0.55 at \(n = 100\).
At \(n = 200\) it increases power from
0.57 to
0.84.

The test-then-adjust strategy's power
(0.32 at \(n = 100\),
\(\gamma = 0.7\)) is close to the unadjusted power and far
below ANCOVA's. On power, therefore, test-then-adjust
behaves like the unadjusted analysis and not like an
intermediate strategy. Its coverage, however, is
systematically above nominal. At \(n = 100\),
\(\gamma = 0.7\), coverage is
0.969
(Monte Carlo SE 0.002).
At \(n = 200\) it is 0.971.
Both values are several Monte Carlo standard errors
above 0.95.

The over-coverage is a selection effect. The analysis
is chosen by conditioning on an ancillary statistic,
the observed imbalance in \(X\)\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-permuttImbalance1990}{46}}.
The point estimate stays unbiased, but the inference
is conditional on the selection event. A short
large-sample argument shows the mechanism. Write
\(D_X = \bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_0\), which has variance
\(4/n\). The unadjusted estimate is
\(\hat{\tau}_U = \tau + \gamma D_X + D_\epsilon\), with
\(D_\epsilon\) independent of \(D_X\) and of variance
\(4(1 - \gamma^2)/n\). Treat the balance test as a
\(z\)-test at \(\alpha = 0.10\) with critical value
\(c = 1.645\). It accepts balance
with probability about
0.90. Given acceptance,
\(D_X\) is a normal variable truncated to
\(|D_X| < c\sqrt{4/n}\). Its variance shrinks by the
factor
\(\kappa = 1 - 2c\,\phi(c)/\{2\Phi(c) - 1\} =
0.623\). The conditional
variance of \(\hat{\tau}_U\) is therefore
\((4/n)\{\gamma^2\kappa + 1 - \gamma^2\}\). At
\(\gamma = 0.7\) and \(n = 100\) this gives a conditional
SD of about 0.181.
However, the unadjusted model SE still estimates the
unconditional value of about \(\sqrt{4/n} = 0.200\) (mean
model SE in the table
0.200). In the
accepted stratum the nominal 95\% interval therefore
covers with probability about
0.970. The ratio does not
depend on \(n\), so the same value applies at \(n = 200\).
The remaining replications use ANCOVA. If ANCOVA covers
at about the nominal rate there, overall coverage is
about 0.968. This agrees
with the simulated values above.

This argument also explains the gap in the table
between the empirical SE of test-then-adjust
(0.177) and its mean
model SE (0.195). Both
are test-then-adjust quantities. Neither is ANCOVA's,
whose empirical SE in this cell is
0.145. The strategy
is therefore dominated by always-adjust. It sacrifices
power relative to ANCOVA and offers no validity
advantage. We note that ANCOVA's coverage is close to
nominal in every scenario studied.

advantage of standardization over the unadjusted

working models are correctly specified. The `correct'
model still fits an unneeded \(X^2\) term, so it is
slightly over-fitted. The two estimates are numerically
close, as expected.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsection{Summary of the criteria}\label{summary-of-the-criteria}

We propose the following criteria for covariate
adjustment in the primary analysis of an RCT. They are
based on the theoretical literature, empirical
evidence, and regulatory guidance reviewed above. Each
states a rule and the conditions under which it
applies. Where a numerical threshold exists, we give
it.

\textbf{When to adjust:}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  Adjust if, at the design stage, at least one
  covariate is known from prior data or clinical
  knowledge to be prognostic for the primary outcome.
  Write the adjustment into the protocol or analysis
  plan before unblinding. The decision should never
  depend on observed baseline balance\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-sennTestingBaselineBalance1994a}{2},\citeproc{ref-fdaCovariateAdjustment2023}{25},\citeproc{ref-sennSevenMyths2013}{33}}.
\item
  \emph{Always} include stratification variables used in
  the randomization design as covariates in the
  analysis\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-kahanImproper2012}{5},\citeproc{ref-emaBaselineCovariates2015}{24},\citeproc{ref-fdaCovariateAdjustment2023}{25}}. The standard error
  should also account for the stratified randomization.
  Otherwise it is generally conservative\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-bugniCAR2018}{29},\citeproc{ref-yeMinimization2022}{44}}.
\item
  \emph{Always} include the baseline measurement of the
  outcome variable, if available\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-sennChangeBaseline2006}{7}--\citeproc{ref-yangEfficiency2001}{9}}.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Which covariates to adjust for:}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\setcounter{enumi}{3}
\tightlist
\item
  Select covariates based on prior knowledge of their
  prognostic value. This knowledge may come from previous
  trials, meta-analyses, or established clinical
  understanding\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-raabHowSelectCovariates2000}{6},\citeproc{ref-beachCovariates1989}{41}}.
\item
  Keep the number of covariates \(k\) small relative to
  the sample size \(n\)\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-fdaCovariateAdjustment2023}{25},\citeproc{ref-beachCovariates1989}{41}}. A break-even rule
  follows from standard normal theory for a linear
  model with 1:1 allocation and normally distributed
  covariates. Relative to the unadjusted estimator,
  the expected variance of the ANCOVA estimate is
  multiplied by \((1 - R^2)(n - 3)/(n - k - 3)\). Here
  \(R^2\) is the proportion of within-arm outcome
  variance the \(k\) covariates explain. The factor
  \((n - 3)/(n - k - 3)\) is the mean of
  \(1/(1 - R^2_{Z|X})\), where \(R^2_{Z|X}\) is the chance
  correlation of treatment with the covariates.
  Adjustment therefore helps in expectation when
  \(R^2 > k/(n - 3)\). Take \(n = 100\) and \(k = 5\) as an
  illustration (values chosen by us, not from a
  trial). The covariates then need to explain only
  about 5\% of the outcome variance. The rule is approximate outside
  this setting.
\item
  Do not select covariates based on the trial data
  itself (neither on baseline imbalance nor on
  observed covariate-outcome associations).
  Selection on observed imbalance makes the inference
  conditional on the selection event, as our first
  simulation shows\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-raabHowSelectCovariates2000}{6},\citeproc{ref-beachCovariates1989}{41},\citeproc{ref-permuttImbalance1990}{46}}.
  The FDA asks sponsors to discuss any data-adaptive
  selection procedure with the review division in
  advance\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-fdaCovariateAdjustment2023}{25}}.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{How to adjust:}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\setcounter{enumi}{6}
\tightlist
\item
  For continuous outcomes: use ANCOVA (linear
  regression of \(Y\) on \(Z\) and \(X\)). In two-arm trials
  with 1:1 allocation, model-based (OLS) or robust
  standard errors are both acceptable. With unequal
  allocation or more than two arms, use a robust
  standard error or the bootstrap\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-linAgnostic2013}{11},\citeproc{ref-wangANCOVA2019}{12},\citeproc{ref-fdaCovariateAdjustment2023}{25}}.
\item
  For binary outcomes: fit a logistic regression
  model and compute the marginal effect
  (standardization / g-computation). This yields a
  risk difference or relative risk\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-mooreCovariate2009}{17},\citeproc{ref-freedmanLogistic2008}{27},\citeproc{ref-steingrimssonBinary2017}{28},\citeproc{ref-vanLanckerCovariate2024}{31}}.
  The binary-outcome results above illustrate this
  procedure under one mild misspecification.
  Alternatively, use a linear probability model or
  modified Poisson regression with robust standard
  errors.
\item
  For time-to-event outcomes: use a covariate-adjusted
  log-rank test\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-luLogRank2008}{19},\citeproc{ref-yeLogRank2024}{20}} or
  estimate the restricted mean survival time with
  covariate adjustment\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-diazSurvival2019}{21}}.
\item
  Base inference on a variance estimator that stays
  valid when the working model is wrong. For the
  standardized estimator, this means a sandwich-based
  delta-method SE or the bootstrap. For ANCOVA it
  means a robust SE whenever criterion 7 does not
  allow the model-based one\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-tsiatisCovariate2008}{13},\citeproc{ref-rosenblumRegression2009}{15},\citeproc{ref-rosenblumGLM2010}{16},\citeproc{ref-fdaCovariateAdjustment2023}{25}}. Our simulations do not
  compare robust with model-based SEs, so they do not
  bear on this criterion.
\item
  Pre-specify all covariates and the analysis method
  in the statistical analysis plan. On interaction
  terms the two agencies differ. The EMA guideline
  excludes them from the primary model\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-emaBaselineCovariates2015}{24}}. The FDA guidance
  allows them, provided the primary analysis estimates
  the average treatment effect from the model\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-fdaCovariateAdjustment2023}{25}}. Under 1:1 allocation
  the model without interactions is adequate\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-linAgnostic2013}{11}}. With unequal allocation, the
  model without interactions can be less precise than
  the unadjusted analysis. Lin's fully interacted
  estimator with a robust SE cannot be, asymptotically,
  and it is acceptable to the FDA. A trial filed with
  both agencies may need to justify it to the EMA.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Consistency with regulatory guidance}\label{consistency-with-regulatory-guidance}

The criteria agree with both the EMA guideline\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-emaBaselineCovariates2015}{24}} and the FDA final guidance\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-fdaCovariateAdjustment2023}{25}} on the main points. Both
agencies endorse pre-specified adjustment for
prognostic covariates as a tool for improving
precision. Both expect stratification variables to be
included. The agencies differ in two places. First, the
FDA allows treatment-by-covariate interactions in the
primary model, and the EMA does not (criterion 11).
Second, the EMA says that methods selecting covariates
by their association with the outcome in the current
data should be avoided in confirmatory trials. The FDA
does not forbid data-adaptive covariate selection. It
asks sponsors to discuss such proposals in advance.
Criterion 6 follows the EMA and is stricter than the
FDA text on this point. For nonlinear models, the FDA names
standardization as one reliable method for an
unconditional effect. It also allows conditional
effects from nonlinear regression, after discussion
with the review division. The FDA guidance also asks
that standard errors account for stratified
randomization (criterion 2).

\subsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}

The simulations presented here are deliberately simple
and illustrative. They are not a comprehensive
evaluation of the eleven criteria. Both are restricted to
a single covariate and no missing data, and neither
varies baseline imbalance as a design factor. The
continuous-outcome scenario uses a correctly specified
linear data-generating model throughout. The
binary-outcome scenario adds one misspecified working
model (a quadratic term omitted from the covariate) and
one comparator estimator (standardization). This
illustrates criterion 8 but does not test it, and it
says nothing about criterion 10. It does not
examine alternative link functions or multiple
covariates. Nor does it examine more severe or
qualitatively different (e.g., non-monotone,
interaction-driven) forms of misspecification. Finally,
it omits the propensity-score and augmented estimators
discussed in the theoretical foundations section.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-tackneyMisspecification2023}{48}} give a much richer
comparison of these estimators under misspecification.
Neither scenario includes a stratified randomization,
so criterion 2 is not illustrated either. Neither
scenario includes a time-to-event outcome. As a
result, criterion 9 (survival outcomes) still rests on
the cited theoretical and applied literature, not on
evidence generated here. Real trials involve multiple
covariates, various outcome types, missing data, and
complications such as noncompliance and intercurrent
events. The estimand framework of ICH E9(R1)\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ichE9R1Estimands2019}{23}} introduces additional
considerations about what exactly is being estimated.

We note that the broader literature cited throughout
this paper supports the core qualitative message across
outcome types and regulatory frameworks. First,
pre-specified covariate adjustment improves efficiency
without sacrificing validity. Second, standardization
remains valid under working-model misspecification in a
randomized trial. The two scenarios simulated here
illustrate these points for a correctly specified
continuous-outcome model and for a binary outcome under
one mild form of working-model misspecification. The
time-to-event case (criterion 9) remains supported only
by the cited literature, not by simulation evidence
generated here.

The simulation uses heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC2)
standard errors for the ANCOVA and test-then-adjust
strategies, computed via \texttt{sandwich::vcovHC()}. This
matches the Methods description. The FDA guidance
accepts model-based standard errors for two-arm trials
with 1:1 allocation and asks sponsors to consider
robust ones elsewhere. For the
balanced, correctly specified designs considered here,
HC2 and classical OLS standard errors are asymptotically
equivalent\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-linAgnostic2013}{11}}. We retained the sandwich
estimator because it remains valid under
misspecification and heteroscedasticity. Both can arise
in practice. Neither is present by construction in this
simulation.

\section{Future research}\label{future-research}

Several directions for future investigation emerge from
this review.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Machine learning for covariate adjustment.} The
  FDA guidance does not yet address the use of machine
  learning methods for constructing prognostic scores.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-schulerPrognostic2022}{39}} showed that a prognostic score
  trained on external data can achieve the efficiency
  bound. However, the operating characteristics of
  cross-validated or penalized approaches in the trial
  data itself remain under study.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-vanLanckerGroupSequential2025}{49}} explored combining
  covariate adjustment with group sequential and
  information-adaptive designs.
\item
  \textbf{Missing covariate data.} When baseline covariates
  are missing, the analyst faces a tradeoff. On the one
  hand, adjustment brings efficiency gains. On the
  other, ad hoc handling of missingness brings potential
  bias. Multiple
  imputation and augmented estimators remain active
  research areas.
\item
  \textbf{Cluster-randomized and crossover trials.} The
  theory reviewed here applies primarily to
  individually randomized, parallel-group trials.
  Extensions to cluster randomization and crossover
  designs present additional methodological challenges.
\item
  \textbf{Software tools.} Software already exists for
  much of what is recommended here. In R, \texttt{estimatr}
  implements Lin's regression estimator via
  \texttt{lm\_lin()}. \texttt{RobinCar} implements model-robust
  covariate adjustment under covariate-adaptive
  randomization. \texttt{beeca} implements the standardized
  estimator for binary outcomes, and \texttt{stdReg} and
  \texttt{marginaleffects} provide g-computation more
  generally. All are on CRAN. What remains is to
  compare their variance estimators in small samples
  and to extend them to more outcome types.
\item
  \textbf{Harmonization of conditional and marginal
  frameworks.} Conditional effects are natural for
  clinicians reasoning about individual patients.
  Marginal effects are natural for regulators reasoning
  about populations. The tension between the two remains
  unresolved\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-permuttCovariates2020}{30}--\citeproc{ref-harrellCommentary2024}{32}}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Reproducibility}\label{reproducibility}

This manuscript was rendered with R 4.4.x. The simulation
function \texttt{run\_sim()} lives in \texttt{R/run\_sim.R} as a
documented, exported package function. It is
unit-tested in \texttt{inst/tinytest/test\_run\_sim.R}. It uses
base R \texttt{lm()} for model fitting and \texttt{sandwich::vcovHC()}
(HC2) for the ANCOVA and test-then-adjust standard
errors. It uses \texttt{tibble} for the returned summary.
Reporting uses \texttt{dplyr} and \texttt{ggplot2}, and the manuscript
build uses \texttt{rmarkdown} plus \texttt{bookdown}. We note that
\texttt{MASS} is not used; the single covariate is generated
with \texttt{stats::rnorm()}. The binary-outcome scenario's
simulation function \texttt{run\_sim\_binary()} lives alongside
it in \texttt{R/run\_sim\_binary.R}. It is unit-tested in
\texttt{inst/tinytest/test\_run\_sim\_binary.R}. It uses base R
\texttt{glm(family\ =\ binomial())} for model fitting. The
delta-method standard error of the standardized
contrast combines \texttt{sandwich::vcovHC()} (HC2) with a
numerical (central finite-difference) gradient.
Finally, \texttt{stats::integrate()} computes the true
marginal risk difference from the known data-generating
model.

\textbf{Random-number generator.}
\texttt{analysis/scripts/run\_simulation.R} calls \texttt{run\_sim(...,\ seed\ =\ master\_seed\ +\ scenario\_id)} once per scenario, with
\texttt{master\_seed\ \textless{}-\ 20260305}. As a result, each of the
eight \((n, \gamma)\) scenarios has its own reproducible
seed, set at the top of \texttt{run\_sim()}. There is no
per-replicate reseeding within a scenario. Replicates
within a scenario share one continuous RNG stream,
started from that scenario's seed.
\texttt{analysis/scripts/run\_simulation\_binary.R} follows the
same pattern with its own \texttt{master\_seed\ \textless{}-\ 20260819}. The
six \((n, \gamma_2)\) binary-outcome scenarios therefore
each have their own reproducible seed. These seeds are
independent of the continuous-outcome scenario's RNG
stream.

\textbf{Data and code availability.} Neither simulation is
embedded in this Rmd.
\texttt{analysis/scripts/run\_simulation.R} runs the
continuous-outcome factorial design
(\(n \in \{100, 200\}\), \(\gamma \in \{0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7\}\),
\(B = 5000\)). It saves the combined results, together
with the master seed and a timestamp, to
\texttt{analysis/data/derived\_data/sim\_results.rds}.
\texttt{analysis/scripts/run\_simulation\_binary.R} runs the
binary-outcome factorial design (\(n \in \{100, 200\}\),
\(\gamma_2 \in \{0, 0.4, 0.8\}\), \(B = 2000\)). It saves
its results to
\texttt{analysis/data/derived\_data/sim\_results\_binary.rds}.
This document's \texttt{simulation} and \texttt{simulation-binary}
chunks read those \texttt{.rds} files directly. They stop with
an informative error if either is missing. As a result,
the manuscript can never silently render with stale or
absent results. To regenerate the results, first run
\texttt{Rscript\ analysis/scripts/run\_simulation.R} from the
repository root (roughly one to two minutes on a
laptop). Next run
\texttt{Rscript\ analysis/scripts/run\_simulation\_binary.R}
from the repository root (roughly four to six minutes).
The binary run takes longer because \texttt{glm()} fitting and
the delta-method standard error are substantially more
expensive per replicate than the continuous scenario's
\texttt{lm()} fits. Then re-render this document with
\texttt{bash\ tools/render.sh\ analysis/report/report.Rmd}. The
manuscript source, bibliography (\texttt{references.bib}), and
CSL file are at \texttt{analysis/report/}.
